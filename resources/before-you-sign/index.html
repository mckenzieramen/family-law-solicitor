Before You Sign: What to Ask Your Sydney Family Lawyer About Any Separation Agreement

Melissa Gok

Why the moment before you sign matters most

After a separation, there is often pressure to “just sign something” and move on. An agreement about property, money or children can shape your finances and family life for years, so the questions you ask before you sign matter more than almost anything that comes after.

This guide sets out the questions we encourage every client to ask before signing any separation agreement, whether it’s consent orders, a financial agreement, a parenting plan or an informal arrangement drafted by your former partner.

One point that surprises many people: a divorce does not deal with property or children. A divorce order simply ends the marriage. Property, superannuation, spousal maintenance and parenting arrangements are separate issues that need their own agreement or orders.

12 questions to ask before you sign

1. What type of agreement is this, and is it legally binding?

Not every document carries the same weight. The main options are:

  • Consent orders: orders made by the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia on agreed terms. They are binding and enforceable, and the Court must be satisfied the property orders are just and equitable.
  • Financial agreements: private contracts under the Family Law Act 1975. They are binding only if strict requirements are met, including each party receiving independent legal advice and a signed statement from their lawyer.
  • Parenting plans: written, signed and dated agreements about children. They are not legally enforceable, but the Court may consider the most recent plan if a dispute arises.
  • Informal or handshake agreements: these usually don’t finalise anything. Either party can generally still apply to the Court later.

If you don’t know which one you’re being asked to sign, stop and find out.

2. Does this finally end all financial claims between us?

A well-drafted agreement should leave no loose ends. Ask whether it deals with every asset, debt and superannuation interest, and whether it releases both of you from future claims, including spousal maintenance. Without a formal agreement or orders, your former partner may be able to make a claim against assets you acquire years later.

3. Have we both made full and frank disclosure?

You can’t judge whether a deal is fair without knowing what there is to divide. Both parties have a duty to disclose all relevant financial information, including bank statements, tax returns, business records, trusts, super statements and recent valuations. An agreement built on hidden assets can later be set aside, which means more cost, delay and uncertainty for everyone.

4. Is this a fair outcome, and how does it compare with what a court might order?

The Court looks at the parties’ assets, liabilities and superannuation, their contributions (financial, non-financial and as homemaker and parent), and their future needs, such as care of children, health, age and earning capacity. Since June 2025, the Court must also consider the economic effect of any family violence. A family lawyer can tell you the realistic range of outcomes, so you know whether the offer is reasonable before you agree to it.

5. What happens to superannuation?

Superannuation is treated as property and can be split between separating couples. It is often one of the largest assets, and it is easy to overlook. Splitting super requires specific wording and the super fund trustee usually needs to be notified. If this step is missed, the split may not be carried out.

6. What happens to the home and the mortgage?

If one of you is keeping the home, ask:

  • Can that person refinance the mortgage in their sole name, and by when?
  • Will the lender release the other party from the loan?
  • What happens if refinance isn’t approved: will the property be sold, and who controls the sale?
  • Who pays the mortgage, rates and insurance in the meantime?

Transferring a title doesn’t automatically remove you from a joint loan. You could remain liable for a debt on a property you no longer own.

7. What are the tax and stamp duty consequences?

A transfer that looks equal on paper may not be equal after tax. Ask about capital gains tax on investment properties and shares, whether rollover relief applies, and whether the transfer qualifies for a NSW stamp duty exemption. Speak with your accountant before signing where tax is involved.

8. How are the children’s arrangements covered?

Ask whether parenting arrangements are recorded in a parenting plan or in consent orders, and whether the arrangements are practical as the children grow, including school holidays, special occasions, travel and changeovers. The Court’s focus is always the best interests of the children.

Child support is dealt with separately, under its own legislation. A binding child support agreement also requires each parent to get independent legal advice.

9. Is spousal maintenance included or excluded?

If one party earns significantly less, or has given up work to care for children, spousal maintenance may be relevant. Make sure the agreement clearly says whether maintenance is payable, for how long, or whether both parties give up the right to claim it.

10. Are there time limits I need to know about?

Yes. If you were married, you generally have 12 months from the date your divorce order takes effect to file for property orders. If you were in a de facto relationship, you generally have two years from separation. After these deadlines, you need the Court’s permission, which isn’t guaranteed.

11. What happens if my former partner doesn’t follow the agreement?

Consent orders can be enforced through the Court. A financial agreement can also be enforced, but only if it was properly made. An informal agreement may give you very little protection. Ask what your options are if payments aren’t made, property isn’t transferred, or deadlines are missed.

12. Am I signing freely, with enough time to understand it?

You should never feel rushed, pressured or threatened into signing. If you’re being told “sign today or the offer is gone”, or you feel unsafe, speak with a lawyer first. Pressure, duress and unfair conduct can be grounds to challenge an agreement later, but it’s far better to get it right the first time.

Red flags to watch for

Get legal advice before signing if any of these apply:

  • Your former partner’s lawyer drafted the agreement and you haven’t had your own advice.
  • You haven’t seen bank statements, tax returns or valuations.
  • Superannuation, a business, a trust or an inheritance isn’t mentioned.
  • You’re being told there’s no need for lawyers because “it’s simple”.
  • There’s a deadline that leaves you no time to think.
  • You’ve experienced family violence, controlling behaviour or financial abuse.
  • You don’t fully understand what you’re giving up.

Your pre-signing checklist

  • I know what type of agreement this is and whether it is binding.
  • All assets, debts and superannuation are listed and valued.
  • We have exchanged full financial disclosure.
  • I understand how the outcome compares with what a court may order.
  • The home loan, refinance and sale steps are clear.
  • I have considered tax and stamp duty, with my accountant if needed.
  • Parenting and child support arrangements are addressed.
  • Spousal maintenance is dealt with.
  • I know the time limits that apply to me.
  • I have had independent legal advice.
