Emergency Parenting Decisions After Separation: What Happens in the First Week

Melissa Gok

Why the first week matters

The days immediately after a separation are often the most stressful for parents and the most unsettling for children. Decisions made in that first week, about where the children live, who collects them from school and how parents communicate, can quickly become the “new normal”. That pattern can be hard to change later.

This guide explains what happens in the first week after separation, what you can and can’t do without court orders, and when you may need urgent help from a family lawyer.

If you or your children are in immediate danger, call 000.

Who makes decisions when there are no court orders?

Until a court makes orders, each parent generally has parental responsibility for their children. That means either parent can usually make day-to-day and major decisions, and neither parent automatically has the final say.

There is no rule that children must spend equal time with each parent, and since May 2024 there is no longer a presumption of equal shared parental responsibility. When the Court makes parenting orders, its focus is the best interests of the child, with the child’s safety a priority.

What to do in the first week

1. Put safety first

If there has been family violence, or you fear for your safety or your children’s safety, contact the police. In NSW, police can apply for an Apprehended Domestic Violence Order (ADVO) to protect you and your children. You can also contact 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) for confidential support, 24 hours a day.

2. Keep the children’s routine as stable as possible

Children cope best when school, childcare, activities and bedtimes stay as familiar as possible. Avoid moving the children to a new suburb or school without the other parent’s agreement, unless safety requires it.

3. Agree on interim arrangements, in writing if you can

If it is safe to do so, try to agree on short-term arrangements:

  • where the children will live and when they will see the other parent
  • who handles school drop-offs and pick-ups
  • how and when changeovers happen
  • how you will communicate about the children

Confirm what you agree to by text or email. A clear written record avoids misunderstandings and can be helpful later.

4. Keep communication calm and child-focused

Keep messages brief, polite and about the children. Avoid arguing in front of the children or using them to pass on messages. Anything you write may later be read by a judge.

5. Keep a diary

Write down key events as they happen: dates, times, what was said, missed changeovers and any concerns about the children’s welfare. Contemporaneous notes are far more reliable than memory.

6. Think about what the children are told

Where possible, both parents should tell the children together, in an age-appropriate way, that the separation is not their fault and that both parents love them.

7. Get early legal advice

A family lawyer can explain your rights and responsibilities, help you set up workable interim arrangements, and tell you quickly whether your situation needs urgent court action.

When you may need to go to court urgently

Most parenting disputes should first go through family dispute resolution (mediation) before a court application is filed. There are exceptions, including urgency, family violence and risk of child abuse. In those cases, you can apply straight to the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia.

The other parent has taken the children and won’t return them

If a child has been taken or is being kept from you, the Court can make a recovery order requiring the child to be returned. Police can be directed to help locate and return the child. Don’t try to take the children back by force. Get legal advice straight away.

You fear the children will be taken overseas

If you’re worried the other parent may take the children out of Australia, act quickly:

  • The Court can make orders restraining a child’s removal from Australia and have the child placed on the Family Law Watchlist, which is monitored at international departure points by the Australian Federal Police.
  • You can ask the Australian Passport Office to place a Child Alert on the child to help prevent an Australian passport being issued without your consent.

There are serious safety concerns

Where there is family violence, abuse or a serious risk to a child, the Court can make interim parenting orders quickly. In urgent cases, it can make them without the other parent being present at first. Interim orders set temporary arrangements, such as who the children live with, supervised time and restrictions on contact, until the matter is finally decided.

Interim orders are temporary, but they matter

Interim orders are designed to protect children while a case runs, which can take many months. Because they often shape the arrangements for a long time, it is important to get your application right the first time and to support it with clear, factual evidence.

Common first-week mistakes to avoid

  • Moving away with the children without agreement or legal advice. A unilateral relocation can count against you later.
  • Stopping the other parent from seeing the children without a genuine safety reason.
  • Posting about the separation on social media. Posts can be screenshotted and used as evidence.
  • Discussing the dispute with the children or asking them to choose between parents.
  • Agreeing to long-term arrangements in the heat of the moment that you can’t sustain.
  • Waiting too long when there is a genuine risk to the children’s safety or of them being taken overseas.

Your first-week checklist

  • The children are safe, and police have been contacted if needed.
  • Interim care arrangements are agreed and confirmed in writing.
  • School and childcare know who is collecting the children.
  • Communication with the other parent is calm, brief and in writing.
  • I have started a diary of key events.
  • Passports and important documents for the children are secure.
  • I have contacted Services Australia about child support and family payments, if relevant.
  • I have spoken with a family lawyer.
