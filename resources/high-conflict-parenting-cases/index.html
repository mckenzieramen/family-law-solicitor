High-Conflict Parenting Cases: Does Conflict Mean Less Time With Your Child?

Melissa Gok

The short answer

Conflict between parents does not automatically mean less time with your child. Most separating parents disagree about something, and the Court understands that. What matters is how the conflict affects the child, and how each parent behaves in the middle of it.

A parent who keeps the child out of the conflict, communicates respectfully and supports the child’s relationship with the other parent is usually in a far stronger position than one who doesn’t.

How the Court decides parenting arrangements

In every parenting case, the child’s best interests are the paramount consideration. When deciding what is in a child’s best interests, the Court considers matters including:

  • the safety of the child and of each person who cares for the child, including safety from family violence, abuse and neglect
  • any views the child has expressed
  • the child’s developmental, psychological, emotional and cultural needs
  • each parent’s capacity to provide for those needs
  • the benefit to the child of a relationship with each parent and other important people, such as grandparents, where it is safe
  • anything else relevant to the child’s circumstances

There is no starting point of equal time, and no presumption of equal shared parental responsibility. Each case turns on its own facts.

When conflict can affect the time you spend with your child

Research consistently shows that children are harmed when they are exposed to ongoing, intense conflict between their parents. The Court may limit or restructure arrangements where conflict is putting the child at risk, for example where:

  • arguments happen in front of the child, especially at changeovers
  • the child is used to pass on messages or asked to take sides
  • one parent speaks negatively about the other to or near the child
  • a parent repeatedly undermines the other parent’s relationship with the child
  • a parent won’t follow agreed arrangements or court orders
  • the child shows signs of stress, anxiety or distress linked to the conflict

Even then, the Court’s response is not always to reduce time. It may instead change how time happens: changeovers at school or a neutral place, fewer handovers, communication only in writing, or the use of a children’s contact service.

Conflict is not the same as family violence

It’s important to distinguish between ordinary disagreement and family violence. Family violence includes physical violence, threats, intimidation, coercive control, and emotional or financial abuse. It is treated very seriously, and the child’s safety comes first.

Where there are genuine safety concerns, the Court can order supervised time, restrict contact or make other orders to protect the child. If you are worried about your child’s safety, get legal advice promptly. If there is immediate danger, call 000.

Who helps the Court understand a high-conflict family?

In high-conflict cases, the Court may:

  • order a family report or child impact report, prepared by a family consultant or psychologist who meets with the parents and children
  • appoint an Independent Children’s Lawyer to represent the child’s best interests
  • refer the parents to parenting programs or post-separation courses

How you present to these professionals matters. Stay calm and child-focused, and be honest about your own part in the conflict.

Practical ways to reduce conflict and protect your case

  1. Keep the children out of it. Never argue, discuss the case or criticise the other parent in front of them.
  2. Communicate in writing. Use text, email or a co-parenting app. Keep messages brief, factual and polite, and write as if a judge will read them.
  3. Make changeovers low-key. Use school, childcare or a neutral public place, and keep handovers short.
  4. Follow agreements and orders. Be on time and consistent. If you can’t comply, tell the other parent early and in writing.
  5. Support the child’s relationship with the other parent, unless there is a genuine safety concern.
  6. Stay off social media about your separation, your former partner or your case.
  7. Keep a factual diary of missed visits, incidents and concerns, recorded without exaggeration.
  8. Consider parallel parenting. Where cooperation isn’t possible, clear and detailed orders can reduce the need for contact between parents.
  9. Get support for yourself. Counselling or a parenting course can help you manage stress, and shows the Court you are focused on your child.
  10. Try family dispute resolution. Mediation can often reduce conflict and reach an agreement without going to court, unless there are safety concerns.

What if the other parent is the one causing conflict?

You can’t control the other parent’s behaviour, but you can control your own. Responding calmly, keeping records and staying child-focused will put you in the strongest position. If the conflict is affecting your child, a family lawyer can advise whether parenting orders, or changes to existing orders, are needed.
