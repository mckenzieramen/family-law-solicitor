Divorce in Australia

A divorce formally ends a marriage. For most people the process is straightforward, but separation dates, service on a spouse and the time limits that follow a divorce can all cause problems if they are not handled properly.

Prevail Family Law can advise you on whether you are eligible, prepare and file your application, and make sure your property and parenting arrangements are protected. This guide covers:

Eligibility

Before you can apply for a divorce in Australia, the Court needs to be satisfied of a few things. Most people meet them easily. Where they don’t, it’s usually because of the separation date, a period of living together, or a question about the connection to Australia.

The three requirements

  • Your marriage has broken down irretrievably. This is the only ground for divorce in Australia, and you don’t need to prove fault.
  • You have been separated for at least 12 months. You can file once 12 months and one day have passed since you separated.
  • There is no reasonable likelihood of reconciliation.

Separation doesn’t need your spouse’s agreement. It can start when one of you decides the marriage is over and acts on that decision, for example by telling the other person or moving out.

A connection to Australia

At least one of you must, on the day you file:

  • be an Australian citizen (by birth, descent or grant)
  • live in Australia and regard it as your permanent home, or
  • have ordinarily lived in Australia for the 12 months before filing.

You don’t need to have married in Australia.

Separated under one roof

You can be separated while still living in the same home. Many couples are, for financial reasons or for the children. You will need to file affidavits, usually from you and from a friend or family member, showing how your relationship changed: separate bedrooms, separate finances, no shared social life, and telling family and friends.

If you got back together for a while

You can resume living together once, for up to three months, without starting the 12 months again. The time apart before and after counts towards the 12 months; the time together doesn’t. If you reconciled for longer than three months, or more than once, the 12 months runs from your final separation.

Married less than two years

Since 10 June 2025, you no longer need to attend counselling or file a counselling certificate. You can apply on the same basis as anyone else.

Children under 18

If there are children of the marriage under 18, the Court must be satisfied that proper arrangements have been made for their care, welfare and development before it grants the divorce. The application needs to describe those arrangements.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I apply before we’ve been separated for 12 months?

No. You can file once you’ve been separated for 12 months and one day. You can prepare your application in advance, and you can resolve property and parenting at any time after separation.

Does my spouse have to agree that we’ve separated?

No. Separation can start when one of you decides the marriage is over and makes that clear to the other, by words or actions.

What if we’re not sure of the exact separation date?

Use your best estimate and be consistent. If your spouse disagrees with the date, or it is close to the 12-month mark, get advice before filing. A disputed date can delay the hearing.

We married overseas. Can we divorce in Australia?

Usually, yes. A marriage that was valid where it took place is generally recognised in Australia. At least one of you must have the required connection to Australia.

Do I need my marriage certificate?

Yes. If you don’t have it, you can order a copy from the registry of births, deaths and marriages where you married. A certificate that isn’t in English needs a translation and a translator’s affidavit.

Procedure

Most divorces follow the same path, from preparing the application to receiving your divorce certificate. Here’s what happens at each step.

1. Check your eligibility and dates

Confirm your separation date, whether you lived under the same roof or reconciled at any point, and that at least one of you has the required connection to Australia.

2. Decide: joint or sole application

You can apply jointly with your spouse, or on your own. A joint application avoids the need to serve your spouse, but both of you must sign it. If your spouse won’t cooperate or can’t be contacted, a sole application is the way forward (see Sole Divorce Application).

3. Gather your documents

  • your marriage certificate (with a translation and translator’s affidavit if it isn’t in English)
  • proof of citizenship or residence, if needed
  • any affidavits about separation under one roof or a reconciliation.

4. File the application

Divorce applications are filed online with the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia through the Commonwealth Courts Portal, and the Court’s filing fee is paid at the same time. The filing fee changes on 1 July each year. The Court then allocates a hearing date, usually a few months away.

5. Serve your spouse (sole applications)

Your spouse must be served with the application and given enough notice before the hearing. Proof of service is then filed with the Court.

6. The hearing

A Registrar decides the application. Most people don’t need to attend. You will generally only need to if you ask to, if your spouse files a response, or if the Court directs you to. Hearings are usually held by phone or video.

7. The divorce order takes effect

If the Registrar is satisfied, they make a divorce order. It takes effect one month and one day later. You can then download your divorce certificate from the Commonwealth Courts Portal and are free to remarry.

After the divorce

The divorce doesn’t resolve property, spousal maintenance or parenting. Once it takes effect, you have 12 months to start property or spousal maintenance proceedings (see Statutory Time Limits).

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a divorce without a lawyer?

Yes. Many people file their own application. A lawyer is worth having where things aren’t simple: separation under one roof, a reconciliation, a spouse who is overseas or can’t be found, or property and parenting still to resolve.

How much does a divorce cost?

There is a Court filing fee, which changes on 1 July each year. You can check the current fee on the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia website. Legal fees are additional. At Prevail Family Law, you will receive a written estimate of costs before any work starts.

How long does it take?

The hearing is usually listed a few months after filing, and the divorce takes effect one month and one day after it is granted. Most divorces take around three to four months from filing.

Will I need to go to court?

Usually not. You will generally only need to attend if you ask to, if your spouse files a response, or if the Court directs you to. Hearings are held by phone or video.

Can I stop a divorce once it’s been filed?

Yes. The applicant can discontinue the application before the hearing. If the divorce order has been made but hasn’t taken effect, and you have reconciled, you can ask the Court to cancel it.

When can I remarry?

Only once the divorce order has taken effect, one month and one day after it is made.

Statutory Time Limits

Getting divorced starts a clock running on your financial claims. Miss the deadline and you may lose the right to a property settlement altogether, or face a costly application to the Court just to be heard.

Property settlement and spousal maintenance: 12 months

If you were married, you must start court proceedings for property settlement or spousal maintenance, or file an application for consent orders, within 12 months of your divorce taking effect. The clock starts on the date the divorce takes effect, not the date you separated.

You can resolve property before you divorce. For many people that’s the safest approach.

De facto relationships: 2 years

De facto couples don’t divorce. Instead, property and maintenance claims must be started within two years of separation.

Missing the deadline

After the time limit, you need the Court’s permission (called “leave”) to start proceedings. The Court will generally only grant it if:

  • you, or a child of the relationship, would suffer hardship if permission were refused, or
  • for spousal maintenance, you could not support yourself without an income-tested pension, allowance or benefit when the time limit expired.

Even then, the Court looks at the length of and reasons for the delay and whether your claim has real merit. Permission is never guaranteed.

Parenting matters: no deadline

There is no time limit for parenting orders. You can apply at any time while a child is under 18.

Responding to a divorce application

If you’ve been served with a divorce application and want to oppose it or correct the facts in it, you generally need to file a response within 28 days of being served, or 42 days if you were served overseas.

Don’t forget your will

Divorce in NSW generally cancels gifts to your former spouse in your will and their appointment as executor, but leaves the rest of the will in place. Superannuation nominations and life insurance beneficiaries are not changed automatically. Review them when you separate.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does the 12-month time limit start?

On the date your divorce takes effect, which is one month and one day after the divorce order is made. It does not start from your separation date.

Do we have to sort out property before we divorce?

No, but it’s often the safest approach. Resolving property first means the time limit never becomes an issue.

Does the time limit apply if we agree on property?

Yes. An application for consent orders must also be filed within the time limit. Many couples finalise their agreement before the divorce takes effect for this reason.

Is there a time limit for child support?

Child support is separate from property and is handled by Services Australia. You can apply for an assessment at any time, but it generally only applies from the date you apply, so don’t delay.

What should I do if the deadline has passed?

Get advice quickly. The longer the delay, the harder it is to persuade the Court to grant permission. The Court will want to know why you didn’t act in time and whether your claim has real merit.

International Divorce

Living overseas, married overseas or have a spouse in another country? You can usually still divorce in Australia. Here’s what changes when a marriage crosses borders.

Who can apply

You don’t need to live in Australia or have married here. At least one of you must:

  • be an Australian citizen
  • live in Australia and regard it as your permanent home, or
  • have ordinarily lived in Australia for the 12 months before filing.

You must also meet the usual requirements: 12 months’ separation, and no reasonable likelihood of reconciliation.

Marriages that took place overseas

A marriage that was valid in the country where it took place is generally recognised in Australia. If your marriage certificate isn’t in English, you will need it translated and an affidavit from the translator. Depending on your circumstances, you may also need to show proof of your citizenship or residence.

If your spouse lives overseas

For a sole application, your spouse must be served at least 42 days before the hearing. Service overseas can take time and may need to follow the laws of that country or an international convention. Start early so the hearing isn’t adjourned.

Attending from overseas

Most people don’t need to attend the divorce hearing. Where attendance is needed, hearings are usually held by phone or video, so living overseas is rarely a barrier.

Divorces granted overseas

A divorce granted in another country is generally recognised in Australia if one of you had a real connection to that country. If you’ve already divorced overseas, you can’t divorce again here, but you may still be able to bring property or parenting claims in Australia.

Property and children across borders

An Australian divorce doesn’t settle property, and international property raises extra questions: which country’s court should decide, and how orders are enforced overseas. The 12-month time limit for property claims still applies once an Australian divorce takes effect. If children live in, or may be taken to, another country, get advice early.

Frequently Asked Questions

I’m living overseas. Do I need to come back to Australia?

No. The application is filed online, and if you need to attend the hearing, it is usually held by phone or video.

Will my Australian divorce be recognised overseas?

That depends on the laws of the other country. If you plan to remarry or live in another country, check how that country treats an Australian divorce.

We already divorced overseas. Do we need to divorce in Australia too?

Usually not. An overseas divorce is generally recognised in Australia if one of you had a real connection to that country. You may still be able to bring property or parenting claims here.

My spouse is overseas and won’t respond. Can I still divorce?

Yes. Your spouse doesn’t need to respond or agree. You do need to prove they were served, or ask the Court to allow another method of service or to excuse service.

Can an Australian court deal with property overseas?

In some cases, yes, if the Court has jurisdiction over you and your spouse. Enforcing those orders overseas can be more complex, so get advice early.

Sole Divorce Application

You don’t need your spouse’s agreement, cooperation or signature to get divorced in Australia. Once you have been separated for at least 12 months, you can file a sole application. This section explains how it works, how to serve your spouse, and what to do when service gets complicated.

Joint or sole: which is right for you?

There are two ways to apply for divorce:

  • A joint application, where you and your spouse prepare, sign and file the application together. There is no need to serve anyone, so it is usually the quicker and simpler option where you are on reasonable terms.
  • A sole application, where you apply on your own. Your spouse is not involved in preparing the application, but they must be formally told about it (served).

A sole application is often the better choice where:

  • your spouse won’t cooperate, or you don’t want to deal with them directly
  • you don’t know where your spouse is
  • your spouse lives overseas
  • there has been family violence
  • you simply want to control the timing yourself.

The eligibility rules are identical either way: 12 months’ separation, no reasonable likelihood of reconciliation, and a connection to Australia.

Step 1: File the application

The application is filed online with the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia through the Commonwealth Courts Portal. Once filed, the Court seals it electronically and gives you a hearing date. Those sealed documents are what you serve on your spouse.

Step 2: Serve your spouse

Service means giving your spouse the sealed court documents, so they know about the case and have the chance to respond. The Court will not grant the divorce without proof that this has happened, unless it has made an order about service.

What to serve

  • the sealed divorce application
  • any affidavits filed with it (for example, about separation under one roof)
  • a blank Acknowledgement of Service (Divorce) form for your spouse to sign and return.

How to serve

  • By post. You can post the documents to your spouse’s address yourself. Service by post is only proven if your spouse signs and returns the Acknowledgement of Service.
  • By hand (personal service). Someone over 18 hands the documents to your spouse. You cannot do this yourself, but a friend, relative or professional process server can. If your spouse won’t sign the Acknowledgement of Service, the person who served them can swear an affidavit confirming service, including how they identified your spouse (for example, from a photo you supplied).

When to serve

Your spouse must be served at least 28 days before the hearing if they are in Australia, or 42 days before the hearing if they are overseas. If service happens later than this, the hearing is likely to be adjourned. Allow extra time for overseas service, which may need to follow the laws of that country.

Step 3: Prove service to the Court

Before the hearing, file your evidence of service: the signed Acknowledgement of Service your spouse returned, and an Affidavit of Service if the documents were served by hand.

If there is no satisfactory evidence of service, the Registrar will usually adjourn the hearing and give you time to serve properly or file better evidence. That means delay, so it pays to get this step right.

When service gets complicated

Service becomes difficult when you don’t know where your spouse lives, your spouse is deliberately avoiding service, or your spouse has received the documents but refuses to sign the Acknowledgement of Service. In these situations, you can apply to the Court for an order for substituted service or dispensation of service.

Substituted service

The Court can allow you to serve your spouse another way, where it is satisfied the documents will come to their attention. Common examples include email or text message, social media messaging, or giving the documents to a family member, friend or employer who is in contact with your spouse.

Dispensation of service

In some cases, the Court can excuse service altogether. This is harder to obtain. You must satisfy the Court that you have made all reasonable efforts to find your spouse and that further attempts would be unlikely to succeed. It is common to ask for dispensation first and, as an alternative, substituted service.

What you’ll need to file

  • an Application in a Case, setting out the orders you are asking for, and
  • a detailed affidavit explaining every step you have taken to find and serve your spouse, with supporting evidence attached (for example, messages, returned mail, search results, and details of contact with family or friends).

Steps that commonly help include contacting your spouse’s family, friends and former employer, checking social media and online searches, engaging a process server or inquiry agent, and checking electoral roll records.

The application is usually heard at the same time as your divorce hearing, and you (or your lawyer) will need to attend. The Registrar may grant the orders, ask for more information, or adjourn the hearing and direct you to take further steps.

What if your spouse responds?

Once served, your spouse can file a Response to Divorce, generally within 28 days of service (or 42 days if served overseas). They might do this to:

  • oppose the divorce, for example because they say you haven’t been separated for 12 months, or that the Court doesn’t have jurisdiction, or
  • correct facts in your application, such as the separation date or the arrangements for the children, without opposing the divorce.

Disagreeing with the divorce itself is not a reason for the Court to refuse it. If a response is filed, you will usually need to attend the hearing.

Will you need to attend the hearing?

Since 10 June 2025, the attendance rules are the same for sole and joint applications. You will generally only need to attend if:

  • you ask to
  • your spouse files a response
  • you have applied for substituted service or dispensation of service, or
  • the Court directs you to attend.

Hearings are usually held by phone or video. If there are children of the marriage under 18, the Court still needs to be satisfied that proper arrangements have been made for them, so describe those arrangements clearly in your application.

After the hearing

If the Registrar grants the divorce, the order takes effect one month and one day later. You can then download your divorce certificate from the Commonwealth Courts Portal. The divorce does not deal with property or parenting, and you have 12 months from the date it takes effect to start property or spousal maintenance proceedings.

Sole divorce checklist

  • Separated for at least 12 months
  • Marriage certificate (with a translation and translator’s affidavit if needed)
  • Application filed and hearing date allocated
  • Spouse served 28 days before the hearing (42 days if overseas)
  • Signed Acknowledgement of Service or Affidavit of Service filed
  • If service isn’t possible: Application in a Case and supporting affidavit filed
  • Will, superannuation and insurance nominations reviewed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I serve the documents on my spouse myself?

You can post them yourself. If they are served by hand, someone else over 18 must hand them over, such as a friend, relative or process server.

What if my spouse won’t sign the Acknowledgement of Service?

If they were served by hand, the person who served them can swear an affidavit of service instead. If they were served by post and won’t sign, you may need to serve them again by hand, or apply for substituted service.

Can I serve my spouse by email or text message?

Only with the Court’s permission. You will need to apply for substituted service and show that the documents are likely to come to your spouse’s attention that way.

Does my spouse have to sign the application?

No. In a sole application, only you sign. Your spouse just needs to be served.

What if my spouse lives overseas?

They must be served at least 42 days before the hearing. Service overseas can take time, so start as early as possible.
