When your separation involves a business, company or trust

If you or your former partner own a business, separation can quickly become more complicated. The business may be your main source of income, your largest asset, or both. It may be held through a company or trust, involve other shareholders or family members, or carry tax consequences if it changes hands.

Prevail Family Law can help you understand how the business, company or trust will be treated in your property settlement, and work towards an outcome that protects both your interests and the business itself.

Is the business part of the property pool?

Generally, yes. A business owned by either party, alone or with others, is usually included in the property pool, whether it was started before, during or after the relationship. This applies whether the business operates as:

  • a sole trader
  • a partnership
  • a company in which either party holds shares
  • a trust, including a family or discretionary trust.

When and how the business was built up, and who contributed to it, is taken into account when assessing contributions.

Companies and trusts

Businesses and assets held through companies and trusts need careful analysis.

Companies. The value of a party’s shares forms part of the property pool. Loans between a party and the company, such as director’s loans, also need to be identified, as they can be an asset or a liability and may carry tax consequences.

Trusts. Although trust assets are not legally owned by either party, the Court can treat them as the property of a party who effectively controls the trust, for example as trustee, director of the corporate trustee, or appointor. Where a party does not control the trust but benefits from it, it may be treated as a financial resource, which can still affect the outcome.

Trust deeds, company constitutions and financial records are usually needed to work out how the structure should be treated.

Valuing the business

A business must be valued before it can be divided. This is often done by an independent expert, such as a forensic accountant jointly appointed by both parties, rather than the business’s own accountant. Common valuation approaches include:

  • Future maintainable earnings, based on the profit the business is expected to continue generating
  • Net assets, based on the value of what the business owns, less what it owes
  • Market value, based on what a willing buyer would pay.

The right method depends on the type and size of the business. A key issue is goodwill: whether the business is worth more than its assets because of its reputation and customer base, and whether that value depends on one person continuing to work in it.

Expert reports also look at how the business is really performing, for example whether personal expenses are being paid through the business or income is being understated.

Options for dealing with the business

There are several ways a business can be dealt with in a property settlement:

  • One party keeps the business and the other receives a greater share of other assets, or a payment to buy them out. This is the most common outcome.
  • The business is sold and the proceeds divided.
  • Both parties stay involved. This is rare and only works where they can continue to work together.

The best option depends on who runs the business, how it is financed, its value, and whether other assets are available to balance the settlement.

If the business is to be sold, the usual steps are:

  1. obtaining a valuation
  2. preparing the business for sale, including its financial records
  3. marketing the business to buyers
  4. negotiating and accepting an offer
  5. completing the sale and dividing the proceeds as agreed or ordered.

A sale can give both parties a clean financial break, but the timing and process should be agreed in writing so neither party is disadvantaged.

Common disputes about businesses

Disputes involving a business usually come down to three issues:

  • Value. One party believes the business is worth more, or less, than the other says.
  • Hidden income. Concerns that business income or benefits, such as cash takings or personal expenses paid by the business, are not being fully disclosed.
  • Control. Disagreement over who will run the business, particularly where both parties have been involved in managing it.

An independent valuation and thorough financial disclosure are usually the most effective ways to resolve these disputes.

Running the business during separation

Separation does not stop the business from operating. During this time, both parties should avoid actions that could reduce the value of the business or prejudice the other party, such as moving assets, changing ownership or drawing unusual amounts from the business.

The Court can set aside transactions designed to defeat a property claim, and can make orders to stop assets being disposed of while the matter is underway.

Disclosure

Business owners have the same duty of full and frank disclosure as every other party, and it usually involves more documents. These can include:

  • tax returns and financial statements for the business, company or trust
  • general ledgers, BAS statements and bank statements
  • trust deeds, company constitutions and shareholder agreements
  • loan agreements and records of director’s or shareholder loans.

Incomplete disclosure can delay settlement, increase costs and lead to orders being set aside later.

Tax and stamp duty

Transferring business assets as part of a property settlement can have tax consequences. In many cases, capital gains tax rollover relief is available for transfers made under court orders or a financial agreement, deferring the tax until the asset is later sold. In NSW, transfers under these orders and agreements are also generally exempt from stamp duty.

Tax outcomes depend on the structure and the assets involved, so advice from an accountant is usually needed alongside legal advice.

Protecting your business

If you own a business, or are about to start one, a financial agreement can set out in advance how it will be dealt with if the relationship ends. Agreements can be made before or during a relationship, or after separation, and are available to both married and de facto couples. They can give business owners, their partners and any co-owners certainty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Find answers to common questions about businesses, companies & trusts.

My business existed before the relationship. Is it still included?

It may form part of the property pool, with earlier ownership relevant to contributions.

Can my former partner make a claim on a family trust?

Possibly, depending on control and the trust’s role as property or a financial resource.

Do we need an expert valuation?

Not always, but an independent valuation can help where value is disputed.

Will I have to sell my business?

Not necessarily. Settlements may be structured to allow the business to continue.

Does unpaid work in the business count?

Yes. Unpaid work can be relevant as a contribution.

What if my former partner controls the records?

Financial disclosure obligations apply and court processes may assist in obtaining records.
