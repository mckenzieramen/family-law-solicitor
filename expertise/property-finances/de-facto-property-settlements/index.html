Separating from a de facto partner

Ending a de facto relationship can be just as difficult as ending a marriage, emotionally and financially. Many people don’t realise that de facto couples, including same-sex couples, have the same rights to a property settlement and spousal maintenance as married couples.

Prevail Family Law can help you understand whether your relationship qualifies, what you may be entitled to, and how to reach a fair division of property.

What is a de facto relationship?

Under the Family Law Act 1975, a de facto relationship is a relationship between two people who aren’t married to each other or related by family, and who live together on a genuine domestic basis as a couple.

There’s no single test. The Court looks at all the circumstances of the relationship, including:

  • how long it lasted
  • whether, and how, the couple lived together
  • whether there was a sexual relationship
  • financial dependence or interdependence, and any arrangements for financial support
  • how property was owned, used and acquired
  • the degree of commitment to a shared life
  • whether the relationship was registered
  • the care and support of children
  • how the couple presented themselves to family, friends and the community.

No one factor is decisive. A couple can be in a de facto relationship without living together full time, and a person can be in a de facto relationship while still legally married to someone else.

Whether a de facto relationship existed, and when it started and ended, is sometimes disputed. These questions can significantly affect a claim, so get advice early if they are in issue.

Can you make a property claim?

As well as showing that there was a de facto relationship, you generally need to show at least one of the following:

  • Length of the relationship. The relationship lasted at least 2 years in total.
  • A child. There is a child of the relationship.
  • Registration. The relationship was registered under state law, such as on the NSW Relationships Register.
  • Substantial contributions. You made substantial contributions to the relationship, and it would be seriously unjust not to make an order.

There are also connection requirements. Generally, one or both parties must live in a participating state or territory, and the relationship must have a sufficient connection with it. NSW is a participating state. Western Australia isn’t: de facto property matters there are dealt with under WA law.

How property is divided

Once a claim can be made, property is divided in the same way as for married couples. The Court identifies the property pool, assesses each party’s contributions, considers their current and future circumstances, and checks that the overall outcome is just and equitable.

The property pool includes everything either party owns or owes, including real estate, savings, superannuation, businesses and debts, whether held in joint or separate names.

Time limit

A de facto property settlement or spousal maintenance claim must be started within 2 years of separation. After that, you need the Court’s permission to apply, which is only given in limited circumstances.

Because the deadline runs from separation, not from any later event, it’s easy to miss. Get advice well before the 2 years are up.

Ways to resolve a de facto property dispute

There are several ways to reach a property settlement:

  • Negotiation and disclosure. Before going to court, parties are expected to exchange financial information and make a genuine attempt to resolve the dispute. Many settlements are reached through negotiation between lawyers.
  • Mediation. An independent mediator helps you and your former partner work towards an agreement.
  • Arbitration. An independent arbitrator hears both sides and makes a binding decision. This can be faster and more private than going to court.
  • Court proceedings. If agreement can’t be reached, either party can apply to the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia. Most cases still settle before a final hearing.

Formalising your agreement

Once you reach agreement, it should be formalised so it is binding. There are two options:

  • Consent orders. These are made by the Court after it reviews your agreement and is satisfied it is just and equitable. They can be enforced through the Court.
  • Financial agreement. This is a private contract that doesn’t need Court approval. Each party must receive independent legal advice before signing, and the agreement must meet strict legal requirements to be binding.

An informal agreement, even if written down, generally won’t stop either party from making a claim later within the time limit.

Protecting yourself in a de facto relationship

If you’re moving in with a partner, or already living together, a financial agreement can set out how property will be dealt with if the relationship ends. This can be particularly useful if one of you owns a home, a business, or expects an inheritance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we have to live together to be in a de facto relationship?

Not necessarily. Living together is an important factor, but the Court considers all the circumstances. Some couples are found to be in a de facto relationship even though they kept separate homes for part of the time.

We were together for less than 2 years. Can I still make a claim?

Possibly, if there is a child of the relationship, the relationship was registered, or you made substantial contributions and it would be seriously unjust not to make an order.

Does a de facto partner get half of everything?

No. There is no automatic 50/50 split. The outcome depends on each party’s contributions and current and future circumstances, just as for married couples.

Is my superannuation included?

Yes. Superannuation is treated as property and can be split between de facto partners.

When does the 2-year time limit start?

From the date of separation. If the separation date is disputed, get advice early, as it can affect whether a claim is in time.

Do I need to register my relationship?

No. Registration isn’t required for a de facto relationship to exist, but it can make it easier to prove the relationship and to meet the requirements for a claim.
